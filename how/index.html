How it works

Every number on these boards comes from a public data source and a documented calculation. Nothing is hand-set. The models rebuild from scratch on a schedule, and the same code produces every figure you see.

⚠ Built from scratch
MLB is the only board that does not start from the betting market. It builds a run projection from individual hitters and the pitcher they face, then converts that to a win probability. That makes it the most independent model here — and the one most able to be wrong.

How a projection is built

1
Wait for confirmed lineups
Only hitters actually starting are projected. No injury feed is needed — an injured player simply isn't in the lineup.
2
Match each hitter to the pitcher
Batter xwOBA vs the starter's xwOBA-against, combined with log5 — the standard method for pitting two rates against each other.
3
Regress the pitcher, add the bullpen
A starter faces roughly 60% of plate appearances; the rest is that team's bullpen. His own rate is regressed toward league average first, because one hot or cold month isn't talent.
4
Sum to team runs, then adjust the environment
Lineup quality becomes expected runs, scaled by a three-year park factor and a home-field term.
5
Convert runs to a win probability
Both teams' run totals are treated as Poisson distributions and every possible scoreline is summed.
6
Compare to the market
FanDuel's price is devigged and the gap becomes the edge shown on the card.

Constants in the code

Starter PA share60%
Pitcher regression0.55
Batter regression0.75
Home field1.035
Park factors3yr

What it doesn't do

  • No defence. Nothing accounts for the fielders behind a pitcher.
  • Umpire is unused. Fetched and displayed, but not yet in the run projection.
  • Poisson runs hot. Treating both teams as independent overstates confidence by roughly 2–3 points at a one-run edge.
  • Not market-anchored, so its edges are larger than the other three and deserve more scepticism.
✓ Market anchored
NFL starts from the posted spread and total, then distributes those points across the roster. Win probability leans on ESPN's FPI, an independent published rating, compared against the devigged price.

How a projection is built

1
Start from the market
Implied team points come straight from the total and spread — (total − spread) / 2. The book's number is the starting point, not something to argue with.
2
Distribute across the depth chart
Those points spread over the roster using ESPN depth-chart order and season usage rates.
3
Redistribute for injuries
Players ruled out have their volume reassigned down the depth chart. The official report adds practice participation — a Questionable who didn't practice is treated differently from one who went full.
4
Adjust for efficiency
EPA+CPOE for quarterbacks, WOPR for receivers, scaled in standard deviations and capped at ±8%. Deliberately small — an efficiency edge is worth a few percent, not thirty.
5
Apply weather
Wind and rain suppress passing volume and totals.

Constants in the code

Efficiency per SD4%
Adjustment cap±8%
League avg total44.5

What it doesn't do

  • No pressure rate or O-line grading. That needs charting data an order of magnitude larger to download.
  • No pace or play-calling model. Neutral pace and pass rate over expectation require full play-by-play.
  • Efficiency weights are priors, not fitted values — chosen to be conservative until results justify more.

How a projection is built

1
Derive team goal rates from the market
The posted total and puck line give each side's expected goals. Without a market, it falls back to expected goals for and against.
2
Identify the likely starting goalie
The goalie with the most appearances, not whoever the roster lists first.
3
Adjust his numbers for context
A backup keeps 55% of his GSAx, a goalie on the second night of a back-to-back keeps 35%, and anyone under eight appearances is halved. Every adjustment pulls toward league average rather than inventing a penalty.
4
Scale every skater by the opposing goalie
Shot and goal probabilities move with the quality of the netminder actually expected to start.
5
Poisson for goals, compare to price
Goal totals convert to win probability and the market gap becomes the edge.

Constants in the code

Backup threshold35%
Backup GSAx kept0.55
Back-to-back kept0.35
Home ice1.05

What it doesn't do

  • Starters aren't confirmed. The most-used goalie is an estimate, not a lineup report.
  • High-danger save% is shown but unused. Blending it into GSAx needs a conversion fitted to real results first.
  • No line-level chemistry beyond power-play unit detection.
✓ Market anchored
NBA takes pace and implied points from the market, then spends most of its effort on the thing that moves basketball numbers hardest: who is available and who absorbs their minutes.

How a projection is built

1
Set the scoring environment from the market
The posted total sets pace; the spread splits it between the teams.
2
Remove unavailable players
Anyone listed out or doubtful is taken off the floor entirely.
3
Redistribute by role, not evenly
Minutes flow to players at the same position; production flows to whoever already produces that stat per minute. A guard sitting sends assists to ball-handlers and rebounds to bigs, not the same share to everyone.
4
Apply fatigue and blowout risk
Second night of a back-to-back trims output; a likely blowout caps starters' minutes.
5
Spread to win probability
A normal distribution with a 13-point standard deviation, which is what NBA final margins actually look like.

Constants in the code

Margin SD13.0
Total SD17.0
Home court2.5 pts
League total226

What it doesn't do

  • No individual matchup data. Defensive assignments aren't modelled.
  • Availability comes from a feed, not beat reporters, so late scratches can be missed.
  • Untested in a live season — the current build has not yet run against a real slate.
Every model runs on a schedule and rebuilds from source each time.
